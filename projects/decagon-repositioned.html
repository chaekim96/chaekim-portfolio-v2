Part 1 · The thought piece

Why Decagon should stop selling customer service.

Think about the last time you disputed a charge.

You didn't need an answer. You already knew the charge was wrong. You needed someone with the authority to do something about it: reverse it, freeze the card, and tell you when the money would come back.

Most support bots can answer "how do I dispute a charge?" They link you to a help article and wish you a great day. Very few can actually file the dispute, apply the bank's policy, issue a provisional credit, and leave behind a record a regulator would accept.

That gap is the whole opportunity. The question isn't whether an AI agent can talk to customers. Everyone's can. It's whether a company trusts it to make the call.

Don't answer the question.
Make the decision.

This is an outside view, written from Decagon's public site and press coverage as of October 2026. Facts are footnoted. Everything else, including the industry plays and the mockups, is my own judgment. Bank and airline names on screens are fictional.

Everything answers tickets now.

Three years ago, an AI agent that resolved most support conversations was remarkable. Today it's a line item. Intercom prices Fin at about $0.99 per resolution, and Zendesk and Salesforce bundle agents into suites customers already pay for.3 Every vendor quotes a resolution rate between roughly 60 and 80 percent, and every one of those numbers is self-reported.

When that's the scorecard, the buyer is the head of support, the question is cost per ticket, and procurement runs a bake-off. The cheapest credible answer wins. That's a bad game for the company with the best engineering.

The game to playDecisions you can trust

  • Comparison: cost of a wrong decision

Where Decagon already wins.

Look at who Decagon serves and the pattern is already there: Chime, Cash App and Square in fintech; American Airlines and Delta in travel; Deutsche Telekom in telecom.1 These aren't companies with simple FAQs. Their conversations end in refunds, rebookings, plan changes and disputes, where a wrong answer costs money or turns into a compliance finding.

The proof points say the same thing. Decagon reports 70 percent resolution at Chime, and $1M in revenue from conversations handled entirely by AI at Hunter Douglas.1 Investors agree it's a serious company, too: it was reportedly valued at $4.5B in January 2026.2

But the homepage still leads with an "AI concierge for every customer." That's a sentence any competitor could write.

Policy is the product.

Decagon's agents follow Agent Operating Procedures: plain-language instructions an operations lead can read and edit, which the agent turns into real steps against real systems.1 Today they're pitched as a nice way to build an agent. I'd make them the center of the story.

A bank's dispute policy, an airline's rebooking rules and a carrier's retention offers are some of the most valuable documents those companies have. If they live in Decagon, versioned, tested and approved, then the agent is just how the policy gets carried out. Switching vendors would mean rewriting how the business runs. That's a moat a help desk can't bundle away.

Prove it before it ships.

The person who blocks an AI agent at a bank isn't the head of support. It's the risk officer, and their question is simple: how do I know what it will do?

Decagon already has the pieces to answer that. It can simulate conversations before launch, run experiments, and watch every live conversation for problems.1 Packaged for a risk team, that becomes the pitch: run a thousand fake disputes against the new policy, see exactly where the agent would deviate, approve it, and get an audit trail for every real one after. Lead with that, and the risk officer stops being the blocker and starts being the champion.

One industry at a time.

A horizontal platform with a vertical go-to-market. The product stays the same, but each industry gets its own procedures, integrations, test suites and audit reports out of the box, so a new customer starts most of the way there instead of at zero. My order:

  1. Fintech and banking first. The logos and the proof already exist, and disputes are the perfect wedge: high volume, clear rules, real money.
  2. Airlines and telecom next. Both prove voice at scale: storm-day rebooking and bill-shock calls.
  3. Insurance and healthcare payers as design partners. The compliance bar is high and sales cycles are slow, which is exactly why winning there builds the moat.
  4. Commerce and gaming as the revenue story. Not a vertical team, but the place to prove support can make money.

Support should make money.

The most strategic number on Decagon's site is the one it buries: $1M in revenue from AI-handled conversations.1 Cost savings get a support leader a bonus. Revenue gets a meeting with the CFO.

Three moves push the story there. Report revenue in the product, from save offers, upgrades and recovered carts. Make voice the headline, because the phone is the most expensive and least automated channel in every regulated industry. And go proactive: when a flight is cancelled or a payment fails, the agent reaches out first and fixes it before anyone has to ask.

How it should price.

If the product is the decision, pricing every outcome at the same rate undersells it. A few ways to price it:

  • Per resolution. Simple and easy to compare. That's exactly the problem: it puts Decagon next to a $0.99 rate card.
  • Per action, tiered by value. A resolved dispute or saved account costs more than an FAQ answer. Harder to explain, but it matches the value.
  • Platform fee plus outcomes. A base for the policy, testing and audit layer, plus tiered outcomes on top.

I'd lead with the third, and publish a starting price. Enterprise buyers don't need a rate card, but a visible floor shortens the first conversation.

The hard questions.

Doesn't narrowing the story lose deals?

Some, at first. The platform stays horizontal; only the pitch, the packs and the sales teams go vertical. A commerce company can still buy Decagon. It just won't be the homepage.

Can Decagon win against Sierra's war chest?

Not on breadth. Sierra is reportedly valued well above Decagon.3 The way to beat a better-funded rival is to go deeper in fewer places, so that in fintech or airlines Decagon is the obvious choice, not the alternative.

Aren't regulated industries too slow to sell into?

Insurance and payer deals can take well over a year. That's why they start as design partners. Fintech and travel carry the growth while the slow verticals build the moat.

What happens when the agent makes a bad decision?

That's the real risk of this strategy: acting is riskier than answering. Approval thresholds, instant rollback and live monitoring have to be real and visible. The pitch only works if the safety is the product, not a slide.

Won't Salesforce and Zendesk just bundle this?

They'll bundle answering. Governed actions on top of a bank's core systems are much harder. And Decagon should be available inside those marketplaces too, as the decision layer on top of the help desk rather than only a replacement for it.

Help desks answer customers.
Decagon should act for them.

The rollout

It doesn't happen
overnight.

A realistic path, built on what Decagon already ships. Each step earns trust for the next one.

Phase 1 · 0–6 monthsNarrow the story

Rewrite the homepage around decisions and three industries. Publish fintech and airline playbooks. Put revenue and audit proof up front. Publish a starting price.

"The decision layer for regulated customer service."
Phase 2 · 6–12 monthsShip the industry packs

Packs for fintech, travel and telecom. Launch proactive outreach. Sign one insurance and one payer design partner. Start training integrators and outsourced call centers.

A new bank goes live on disputes in weeks, not months.
Phase 3 · 12–24 monthsOpen the procedures

Publish a library of operating procedures and certify partners to write them. HIPAA readiness and regional data residency. Tiered outcome pricing.

Partners ship packs Decagon didn't have to build.
Phase 4 · 24 months+Where policy lives

Decagon becomes the system of record for how regulated companies treat their customers, and every channel, human or AI, follows it.
