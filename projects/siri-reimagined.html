Part 1 · The thought piece

Why your next iPhone should ship nearly empty.

I recently needed a teleprompter for a class assignment.

I figured this would be easy. Download a teleprompter app, paste in my script, record, done. Instead, I spent more time than I expected trying to find a decent one. Some apps locked basic features behind subscriptions. Others were free but clunky. A few had way more functionality than I needed.

All I wanted was a box for my script, adjustable scrolling speed, readable text, and a clean interface. So I stopped looking and made one.

I opened Claude Code, told it what I wanted, and within a few minutes I had a working teleprompter. After one more pass on the design, I liked it better than every teleprompter app I had downloaded. It did exactly what I wanted, and it felt like it belonged on my computer. (It's now VoicePrompter.)

But I could only build it because I know how to use those tools. Most people don't, and they shouldn't have to. And it lives on my laptop, while the place most of us need small tools like this is our phone.

That's when it clicked. I didn't want more apps. I wanted my apps. Small, personal tools built around how I live, not around what a company thinks millions of people might want.

Today, making something like that takes a laptop and a coding tool. It should take one sentence.

"Hey Siri,
make me an app."

I first wrote about this idea in a short essay, What's the Point of Preinstalled Apps? This is the longer version: the full argument, then what it could look like.

We don't need more apps.

Our phones are full of apps we downloaded once and forgot. Studies of phone usage keep landing on the same pattern: people open around ten apps a day and around thirty in a month, out of dozens installed.1

Each app is a whole product designed for millions of people. Each of us only needs a slice of it. The rest is clutter we scroll past, pay for, and update.

People have been quietly pushing back for years. Apple has let us delete most built-in apps since 2016, and in the EU you can now remove even Safari, the App Store, and Messages. We're already subtracting. The operating system just hasn't caught up.

Five apps on day one.

Here's the idea. A new iPhone ships with five apps: Siri, Conversations (Messages and Phone in one), Camera (with your photos built in), Music, and Settings. That's the whole box.

Everything else, you make by asking. "Track my runs and how much I spend on coffee after." Siri asks one question if it needs to, then builds the app from Apple's own design system, using the services you've already approved. About thirty seconds later, it's on your Home Screen.

Features you pull, not features you're given.

Today, a company decides what ships and everyone gets the same update. In this world, you add a feature by asking for it, and it exists that afternoon, only for you. Features you stop using fade away. Apps you outgrow ask before they leave. Like a friend's app? They share it, and you remix it for yourself.

A year in, your iPhone looks nothing like anyone else's, because it was built around you.

The App Store becomes a capability store.

Siri can build a habit tracker. It can't build a ride service, a bank, or a music catalog. Those are real businesses with drivers, accounts, and licenses behind them.

So you don't download a ride app. You allow a ride service, and Siri puts a "get a ride" button wherever it makes sense in your apps. The store stops selling screens and starts listing what services can do.

That raises the obvious question, because the App Store is one of Apple's biggest businesses. A few ways it could still pay:

  • Per transaction. A small cut of every ride, order, or booking made through Siri. Closest to today, though services will push back on fees.
  • Premium capabilities. Services charge for advanced abilities, and Apple takes a share. Familiar, but it adds paywalls inside personal apps.
  • Placement. Services pay to be Siri's first suggestion. Easy money that erodes trust fast if Siri starts to feel like an ad.

I'd lead with per transaction. It's closest to how Apple earns today, and it rewards the services people actually use.

What happens to developers.

If Siri builds the screens, what do developers build? The job splits in two. Service makers (banks, airlines, music services) publish what their service can do and the rules for using it. Component makers design premium building blocks Siri can use, like a great map view or a pro-grade photo editor.

This is a real loss of control, and big brands will feel it most. A coffee chain spends millions on its app. Here, it becomes a set of abilities. The answer is branded components: the brand keeps its colors, voice, and signature moments wherever Siri puts them.

There's an upside, too. When thousands of people ask Siri for the same feature, service makers can see it and build it properly. Users become the roadmap, and feature development stops being a guess.

Can an iPhone actually do this?

It's closer than it sounds. Recent iPhone chips match Mac chips on single-core speed. Phones still have less memory and run hot sooner than laptops, so the work gets split. Small, fast models run on the device for quick changes and anything personal. Heavier builds go to Apple's Private Cloud Compute, which Apple says doesn't keep your data. Every app Siri builds runs sandboxed and can't get more access than you approve.

Most of the plumbing already exists. Shortcuts, widgets, and App Intents already let apps tell Siri what they can do. This concept takes that plumbing and makes it the whole operating system.

The hard questions.

Who fixes it when it breaks?

If a service changes, Siri rebuilds the affected part and tells you what changed. The service maker is responsible for its abilities, and Apple is responsible for the app holding together.

How does anyone help you if every app is different?

Apple's design system is the guardrail. Buttons, navigation, and settings behave the same everywhere, even when the layout is yours.

Isn't this a security nightmare?

An app that can read messages and move money is a big target. Every app is sandboxed, every permission is explicit, and anything involving money asks you first.

Why would Apple risk its App Store revenue?

Because someone else will do this first. Owning the place where apps get made is worth more than owning the shelf where they're sold.

Haven't AI devices already failed?

The ones that failed tried to replace the phone. This keeps the phone people already trust and changes what's on it.

Apple made the computer personal.
This makes it yours.

The everyday moments.

Making an app is the headline. These are the flows you'd use every week.

New or changed by Siri

Change a feature

Every app Siri builds has a Siri button. Ask for a change, see exactly what will be different, then keep it or undo it.

1Tap Siri in the appThe Siri button sits next to the tab bar in every app it builds.
2Say what you want"Add a way to split the bill with my running club."
3Preview the changeSiri shows what's new and what it will access before anything changes.
4It's liveUndo stays available, so trying things out is safe.

Share and remix

Apps spread person to person. Sharing sends the design, never your data.

1Share from the Home ScreenPress and hold any app you made.
2A friend gets a previewThey see what it does and what it uses before they take it.
3Siri tailors their copyTheir copy connects to their own accounts and data.
4Now it's theirsSame starting point, different app.

Allow a service

The new version of installing an app. Siri suggests a service where it would help, and you decide how much it can do.

1Siri suggests itRight when it's useful, like a ride before a calendar event.
2You set the limitsClear abilities and permissions, like today's privacy prompts.
3It shows up where it fitsA ride button inside your calendar, not a separate app.

The rollout

It doesn't happen
overnight.

A realistic path, built on what iOS already has. Each step earns trust for the next one.

Phase 1Siri builds widgets and shortcuts

Starts with today's App Intents. Low risk, and it teaches people that asking works.

"Make me a widget for my rent split."
Phase 2Personal apps, next to the App Store

Siri builds full apps that sit beside downloaded ones. Nothing is taken away yet.

Your Home Screen mixes both.
Phase 3Services publish capabilities

Developers ship abilities and branded components. The App Store starts listing them.

Allow a ride service instead of installing it.
Phase 4The five-app iPhone

New iPhones ship nearly empty. You can still restore the classic apps if you want them.
